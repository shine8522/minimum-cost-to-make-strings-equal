Step 2 · Breaking the idea

Now try "abcd" vs "acdb"

Frequency map says: identical. But look again — "abcd" ≠ "acdb". The letter b sits at position 1 in s1, but at position 3 in s2. Frequency counting is blind to position.

The missing ingredient isn't which characters are common — it's whether they appear in the same relative order.

Order preserve karte hue, dono strings mein maximum kya rakh sakte hain?

(Keeping the relative order intact, what's the most we can retain from both strings?)

Step 5 · Naming what you found

This idea already has a name: LCS

What you built — A, C, D — is the Longest Common Subsequence of "abcd" and "acdb". Let's unpack the three words.

Longest

Among every valid order-preserving sequence both strings share, take the one with the maximum length.

len("ACD") = 3 → the winner

Common

The sequence must exist in both strings — not just one.

A, C, D ∈ s1 and ∈ s2

Subsequence

Characters don't need to be contiguous — just in the same relative order.

"acd" from "abcd" — skip b, order kept
Step 6 · From LCS to cost

Delete everything outside the LCS

Once we know the LCS, the answer is mechanical: anything in a string that is not part of the LCS must be deleted — otherwise the strings won't match.

deleted = stringLength − LCSLength
totalCost = deleted1 × cost1 + deleted2 × cost2

s1 = "abcd" (len 4), cost1 = 10

LCS length3
deleted1 = 4 − 31
cost1 contribution = 1 × 1010

s2 = "acdb" (len 4), cost2 = 20

LCS length3
deleted2 = 4 − 31
cost2 contribution = 1 × 2020
Total minimum cost = 10 + 20 = 30

Step 10 · The mismatch decision

Why max() of two branches?

At lcs(1,1): s1[1]='b', s2[1]='c' — mismatch. We can't take both, so we try dropping one character from either side and keep whichever path survives longer.

lcs(i+1, j)
drop s1[i] = 'b'
→ 2
lcs(i, j+1)
drop s2[j] = 'c'
→ 1

We take max(2, 1) = 2 — never both, never neither. This is the single decision repeated at every mismatch in the whole recursion.
